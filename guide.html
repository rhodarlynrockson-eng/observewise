Observation Guide

Practise writing clear, objective observations that describe what a child does and says.

Use fictional examples when practising on ObserveWise. Follow your setting's policies when recording real observations.

Describe what you see and hear

Record actions and spoken words before interpreting them. Avoid guessing a child's feelings or intentions.

Assumption

“Child A was bored and did not like the activity.”

Objective description

“Child A put the paintbrush on the table, said ‘I want to play outside’ and walked towards the door.”

Include the child's voice

Include the child's exact words where relevant, using quotation marks to distinguish them from your description.

If the child did not speak, describe the gestures, expressions or actions you observed. Do not invent a quotation.

Fictional example

Child A pointed to the taller tower and said, “This one is bigger.”

Identify learning and plan next steps

Use the evidence in your observation to suggest what the child may be exploring or learning. Keep your interpretation separate from the description of what happened.

Possible learning

Child A compared the heights of two towers and used the word “bigger” to describe a difference. This may suggest an interest in size and comparison.

Possible next step

Offer blocks of different sizes and invite Child A to build and compare towers, following the child's interest. Introduce words such as “taller” and “shorter” during play.

Areas of learning and development in England

The seven areas below follow the Early Years Foundation Stage (EYFS) in England. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland use different frameworks. Follow the guidance relevant to your nation, course and setting.

Reflect on which areas relate to the evidence you recorded. One observation may connect to more than one area.

  • Communication and language
  • Physical development
  • Personal, social and emotional development
  • Literacy
  • Mathematics
  • Understanding the world
  • Expressive arts and design

Link to the fictional example

Comparing tower heights may connect to mathematics. Using the word “bigger” to explain a difference may also connect to communication and language.

Check your observation

  • Have I described what I saw and heard?
  • Have I separated evidence from interpretation?
  • Are any quotations the child's exact words?
  • Does my suggested learning relate to the evidence?
  • Have I linked development areas to specific evidence?
  • Does the next step follow the child's interests?
  • Have I used fictional information for this practice?
